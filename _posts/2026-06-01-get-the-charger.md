Why do I wait for my laptop to shut down before I grab the charger?

I got the low-battery notification and didn't even snooze it. Just ignored it until the screen went black, and only then did I get up for the charger.

A paper by [Raphaël Le Bouc & Mathias Pessiglione](https://lnkd.in/dvN746kB) provides the specific reason for this: effort is discounted more steeply than reward. This means that while the reward of a charged laptop stays roughly the same in your mind whether you get the charger now or later, the perceived effort of getting up feels much higher if you have to do it immediately. Delaying the task makes the "cost" of the effort appear to shrink, which mathematically tricks the brain into thinking postponement is the "better" value.

Let's take it up a notch. Why does it take a bit longer to finish a thesis?

Our decisions are shaped by predicted rewards and how we weigh them against costs. Working on a future version of yourself, whatever that entails, requires a kind of mental time-travel: vividly imagining your future self and the long-term payoff. This is called episodic prospection, and people who are better at it tend to be more patient with delayed rewards.

If I write 500 words about fairness processing instead of chilling, there's no immediate reward. It's easier to start typing when I can actually picture the finished thesis and how my future self would feel about it. The clearer that image, the smaller the pull of the couch.

The same logic applies to the charger. Getting up from a comfortable chair, walking to the other room, digging through a backpack, is exactly the kind of small discomfort I postpone until there's no other option.

There's a related psychological finding worth knowing: when people imagine their future self as a stranger rather than as continuous with who they are now, they save less, plan less, and procrastinate more.

Procrastination is a habit, and habits unlearn the same way they learn: through repetition of the smaller, less aversive version of the action. Starting "for just two minutes" works because it lowers the perceived cost below the threshold where avoidance kicks in.

Get the charger. Write the paragraph. Book the appointment. Start small, and you never know who you will become.
