I am in a nostalgic mood, because ŌURA just launched a Brain Health Study.

In my fourth year at Glasgow, we were given an open brief: write a grant proposal on anything you like, to show that you could. I chose problematic internet use. The central question was simple, almost too simple: what is a healthy amount of daily internet use, and what does it do to our cognition? I deliberately picked the "how much" question over the "should we use it at all" one. The abstinence framing was still a trendy debate at the time, though never a realistic one.

The design combined smartphone screen-time logs with smartwatch data on sleep, physical activity and sedentary time, then measured memory, concentration, mood and anxiety across different age groups and populations. It even sketched a small intervention, where part of the sample would lower their daily internet time so we could track what changed. At the time, consumer wearables still felt too noisy to count as serious neuroscience.

This month, Oura launched a Brain Health Study through its Oura Labs platform, in partnership with Cambridge Cognition. It pairs validated digital cognitive assessments with the ring's continuous physiological data, aiming to map how sleep, recovery and everyday behaviour relate to cognitive function over time. A week earlier, Oura had filed a draft registration statement with the SEC for a potential IPO.

So the design I once thought was naive is now shipping to millions of fingers, and heading for the public markets. Honestly, that thrills me.

This is the kind of naturalistic, large-scale, longitudinal cognitive neuroscience that was almost impossible until recently. Most of what we know about sleep, cognition and mood comes from small samples studied in artificial lab conditions, often relying on what people remember to report. Continuous passive sensing changes the scale of the questions we can even ask, and it does so in the messy, real environments where cognition actually happens. The weaknesses my student self listed so plainly, small samples, self-reported use, narrow populations, are exactly what wearables are built to overcome.

What excites me most is the shift in who gets to do this kind of science. A question I scribbled into a class assignment is now a feature inside a product worn by millions of people, backed by a respected cognitive-testing partner and an IRB. The instruments of cognitive neuroscience are moving out of the lab and onto the body, opening doors to the very questions my field has struggled to answer about mood, motivation and daily life.

I imagined the questions I once jotted down for a class would be asked at a large scale, and I'm here to witness these milestones while preparing myself to join this dialogue. What a time to be curious about the brain.
